What matters most?

How it works
  1. Sun & Earth, every hour. Positions checked against NASA JPL Horizons.
  2. Real terrain. NASA laser-altimeter maps give each site's skyline — ridges block the Sun and Earth.
  3. Power & heat. Panels, battery and heaters are simulated through every dark spell.
  4. Things go wrong. 200 replays with a late landing, a spot 100 m off target, weaker panels and an aged battery.

Working on it
    Surface mission

    The Moon turns once every 29.5 days, and near the pole the "seasons" change over about a year, so a few months shows the real pattern of light and dark.

    Mission priority

    A rover that drives all day needs power; a lander streaming science needs a steady link to Earth. The weight decides which matters more in the ranking.

    Candidate sites
    Add a site anywhere on the Moon

    South of 75°S, the server computes the site's terrain horizon from the LOLA map when it is installed. Elsewhere Clearway uses a smooth Moon, or a horizon file you import.

    Solar array

    Near the pole the Sun sits just above the horizon, so flat panels catch almost nothing. Polar landers use vertical panels.

    Battery & loads

    Operating mode runs while at least half the Sun is above the skyline; survival mode runs in the dark. With the thermal model on, the survival load is the electronics below plus whatever the heaters need; the fixed survival load is used only when it is off. Usable capacity = nominal × usable depth.

    Landing safety

    Slopes come from NASA PGDA 5 m/pixel maps within 100 m of a site where they exist, otherwise from the LOLA grid (regional slope only). The Monte Carlo counts a touchdown steeper than this limit as a failure.

    Direct-to-Earth link
    Radio link budget & relay

    Planning-grade link equation with the real Earth–Moon distance: required Eb/N0 2.5 dB, 3 dB margin, 3 dB other losses. The relay link counts when the relay is above the real skyline and can see Earth; its orbit is illustrative, not a specific mission.

    South-pole map · NASA data

    This map is made from NASA data: the shading is LOLA laser-altimeter terrain, "Average sunlight" is the share of time each spot sees the Sun over an 18.6-year cycle (Mazarico et al. 2011), and the hatched patches are permanently shadowed regions — cold traps where water ice can survive. Good landing sites sit on bright ridges close to hatched craters.

    3 · Horizon view

    The grey shape is the skyline around the lander, built from NASA laser-altimeter maps. The Sun (white disc) and Earth (grey disc) are drawn at their true size; the dots trace their paths over the whole period. When a disc sinks behind the terrain it gets a red dashed outline: the lander loses power or its radio link — even if the dashed smooth-Moon line says it should be visible.

    6 · Survival under uncertainty

    Monte Carlo

    Real missions never land exactly where and when planned, and real hardware never matches the spec sheet. Here every site's planned landing is replayed hundreds of times with the uncertain inputs drawn at random. A site that survives in nearly every replay is a robust choice; one that only just survives is fragile.
